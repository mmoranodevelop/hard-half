# Signal vs. Noise

## The cut is the whole skill

**Signal** is the 3–5 things that must be finished in the next 18 hours for the mission to move. **Noise** is everything else — email, messages, good ideas, other people's urgency, the tidy-up that feels productive.

Noise is not "unimportant". Some of it is mandatory and some of it is genuinely excellent. It is simply **not what moves the mission today**, and treating it as though it were is how a week disappears with everything touched and nothing finished.

The instinct when someone brings an overloaded list is to help them fit it all in. That instinct is the thing to resist. **Anyone can add. The value here is subtraction**, and it only works if the cap holds — a Signal list of eight items is a to-do list wearing a costume.

Real focus is saying no to ideas you believe are excellent. If nothing was painful to cut, the cut did not happen.

## The horizon

**18 hours** — from now to tomorrow morning.

Short enough that only genuinely finishable things fit, which is what makes the list honest. Long enough to include this evening, so it is not "the rest of the working afternoon". A week-long horizon lets vague projects masquerade as tasks; 18 hours exposes them immediately.

## The ritual

### 1. Dump everything

Ask for all of it, unfiltered and unordered: tasks, ideas, nagging obligations, the message not yet answered, the thing being avoided. No judging, no sorting.

Anything still only in their head keeps consuming attention in the background. Getting it onto the page is what makes the cut possible, and it is why this step comes before any thinking about priority.

When they say they are done, ask once more: **"what is the thing you did not write down?"** The avoided item is usually either the most important Signal or the largest hidden Noise, and it is almost never neutral.

> **Done when:** the list is out, and the follow-up question has been asked and answered.

### 2. Name the mission

One sentence: what outcome does this period serve?

Signal only exists relative to a mission. Without one, "important" has nothing to measure against and the urgent wins by default — because urgency announces itself and importance does not.

Push for an **outcome**, not an activity. "Grow the business" cannot sort a list. "Get the first three paying customers" sorts it instantly.

> **Done when:** the mission is one sentence naming an outcome, and every item on the list can be judged against it.

### 3. Make the cut

Move 3–5 items into **Signal**. Not six.

Each one must pass three tests:

- **Finishable inside the horizon.** If it cannot be finished in 18 hours, it is a project, not a Signal — take the next finishable slice of it instead. This single rule fixes most bad lists.
- **Traceable to the mission.** The user should be able to say "this moves the mission because…" without pausing. A pause means it is Noise that feels productive.
- **An outcome, not an activity.** "Ship the signup page" is checkable. "Work on the signup page" can absorb the entire day and end unfinished.

When more than five survive, ask the tie-breaker: **if only one of these got done, which would make the day worth it?** Rank from there and cut below five.

Expect resistance, and expect it to be reasonable. "But this is important too" is usually true — and irrelevant, because the constraint is not importance, it is the horizon. Every item added past five reduces the odds that any of them finish.

The good ideas that get cut go to Noise with a disposition (step 4). That is what makes saying no survivable: the idea is not lost, it is parked with a date.

> **Done when:** 3–5 items, each finishable and traceable, and the user has let go of at least one thing they wanted to keep.

### 4. Dispose of every Noise item

The Noise column is not a graveyard. An item sitting there undecided still occupies attention — the whole benefit comes from the decision, not the relocation.

Every item gets exactly one disposition:

| Disposition | Meaning | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Delegate** | Someone else owns it | Name the person, and what they need to start |
| **Defer** | It matters, not today | Name a date or the cycle it returns in |
| **Batch** | Mandatory, but not mission work | Goes in a timebox, never the first hours |
| **Delete** | It is not going to happen | Say so plainly and let it go |

**Batch** is the honest category, and it is why this survives contact with a real job. Payroll, a compliance deadline, a customer escalation — mandatory Noise exists. The discipline is not pretending it away; it is refusing to let it take the best hours.

**Delete** is the one people avoid. An item deferred for the fifth time has already been deleted in practice — saying so out loud is the only thing that stops it consuming attention on every future pass.

> **Done when:** zero items remain without a disposition, and every Delegate names a person.

### 5. Protect the ratio

Target **80% Signal, 20% Noise — measured in time, not item count.** Twenty small Noise items can be 20% of a list and 70% of a day.

The rule that carries most of the value: **the first hours of the day belong to Signal #1.** Attention is at its best early and never recovers to that level. Opening email first hands the best hours of the day to other people's priorities, and every messaging app is engineered to make that feel like work.

Noise gets a timebox with a stated end, not "whenever I get a moment".

> **Done when:** the day has a shape where Signal #1 gets the first working block, and Noise has a bounded slot.

### 6. Close the loop

At the end of the horizon, before the next dump: what finished, what did not, and what the results actually say.

**Failure only becomes knowledge if the expectation was recorded.** Without it, a thing that did not work is just a loss — you cannot tell whether the idea was wrong, the execution was wrong, or it simply needed longer. That distinction is the entire value of failing, and it survives only if you wrote down what you expected first.

Three questions:

1. Which Signals finished? For any that did not — was it too big, blocked, or never really Signal?
2. What did the data say about what shipped? Not how it felt — what it did.
3. What is different in the next cycle because of this?

A Signal item that reappears three days running is not a Signal. It is blocked, mis-sized, or being avoided, and the review is where that gets named.

> **Done when:** every Signal is marked finished or not with a one-line reason, and one thing changes in the next cycle.

## The two checks that fire on their own

These do not belong to a step. Raise them the moment the situation appears.

### The scale check

When the user proposes automating, building infrastructure for, or "properly engineering" something nobody has used yet:

**Do things that do not scale.** At the start, the manual version is not a compromise — it is the fastest route to the only thing that matters, which is real feedback from real users. Onboard them by hand. Send the emails yourself. Run the process in a spreadsheet.

Premature scaling is expensive twice: it spends the time, and it removes the direct contact that would have told you what to build. The scaled version of the wrong thing is worse than no version at all.

The question to ask: *what would you learn from ten users done by hand that you cannot learn from the automated version?* Usually the answer is "everything that matters", and it arrives weeks sooner.

Automate when the manual version is provably working and the volume genuinely hurts. Detail: `references/scale-and-experiments.md`.

### The feedback check

When work is shipping steadily but nothing is being learned:

**Produce → publish → attend to the data → repeat.** A cycle that skips the third step feels like progress and produces none, and it is the most common reason someone works hard for months with nothing compounding.

Also worth asking, when the output is for an audience: **does this serve a stranger?** Not the author, not their peers. Something that would have to earn the attention of someone with no reason to care. That test is uncomfortable and it is the one that improves the work.

Detail: `references/feedback-loops.md`.

## When this ritual fails

- **The list is five items but one is "finish the app".** A bundle wearing a single line. Split it and take the finishable slice.
- **Nothing was painful to cut.** The cut did not happen; the user reshuffled. Go back to step 3.
- **The same Signal has appeared for three days.** Blocked, mis-sized, or avoided. Name which, in the review.
- **The Noise column has fifty undisposed items.** It became a graveyard. Every item takes a disposition or leaves.
- **The ritual is being used to avoid the work.** Planning feels productive and costs nothing. It should take ten minutes; if it is taking an hour, that is Noise with a clipboard.
- **The mission changes every day.** Then it is not a mission. Zoom out one level and find the thing that held still.
