# Making the cut

Step 3. The hard part, and the only part that produces the value.

Everything before this is administration. Adding items to a list is easy and feels productive; **removing them is the skill**, and it is the one people cannot do alone — which is exactly why it is worth doing with someone else in the room.

---

## The three tests, applied

### Finishable inside the horizon

The most mechanically useful rule in the whole ritual, because it is objective.

"Redesign the onboarding" cannot finish in 18 hours. It is a project, and projects on a Signal list guarantee the day ends with nothing complete.

The move is not to drop it — it is to take the **next finishable slice**:

| Not finishable | The slice |
|---|---|
| Redesign the onboarding | Write the three screens' copy and get it reviewed |
| Launch the newsletter | Publish issue one |
| Fix the performance problems | Profile the checkout path and identify the top cost |
| Hire an engineer | Write the job description and send it to three people |

Notice what the slices have in common: someone else could tell whether they happened.

### Traceable to the mission

Ask directly: *how does this move the mission?*

Watch the pause. An instant answer means it is Signal. A pause followed by a constructed justification means it is Noise that feels productive — and the constructed justification is itself the tell, because real Signal does not need to be argued for.

The most common false Signal: work that is genuinely valuable but serves a *different* mission. It is not waste, it is misfiled. Defer it to the cycle where that mission is the one being served.

### An outcome, not an activity

"Work on X" can absorb an entire day and end with nothing shipped, and it will still feel like the item was addressed. "Ship X" either happened or it did not.

The rewrite is quick: put the finished state in the line. *Draft* the proposal, *send* the three emails, *deploy* the fix, *decide* the pricing. Verbs that end.

## When more than five survive

They will. Everything on a good list looks essential, because it all got there by being important to someone.

**The tie-breaker:** *if only one of these got done, which one would make the day worth it?*

Then ask it again about what remains. Ranking is easier than cutting — people who cannot delete an item can almost always order two of them, and the ordering does the deleting for you.

**The regret test**, when ranking stalls: *which of these will you be annoyed about in a week if it did not happen?* Regret is a better predictor of importance than urgency, and it is available instantly.

**The subtraction test**, when everything still looks equal: *what happens if this simply does not get done today?* Most items have a genuinely small answer, and hearing yourself say it out loud is what makes the cut possible.

## Handling the resistance

It is worth taking seriously. The objections are usually correct on their own terms — the response is not to argue that the item is unimportant, but that importance is not the binding constraint.

**"But this is important too."**
True, and not the point. The constraint is 18 hours, not merit. Every item past five reduces the odds that *any* of them finish. Ask which of the current five it should replace — that reframes it from *deletion* to *exchange*, which people can do.

**"This will only take five minutes."**
Then it is Noise, and it goes in the batch. The cost of a small task is not its duration; it is the context switch on either side of it, and five of them can consume a morning that nobody can account for afterwards.

**"My boss / client asked for it."**
Real constraint, and it does not automatically make it Signal. If it must happen today it is **mandatory Noise** — batched, timeboxed, and never in the first hours. If it is genuinely mission-critical, it displaces something and the user should say which.

**"It's a brilliant idea, I don't want to lose it."**
It will not be lost. It goes to Noise with a **Defer** and a date. That is the mechanism that makes saying no survivable — the fear is losing the idea, not doing it today, and a parked idea with a date answers the actual fear.

**"I can't cut anything."**
Then the mission is wrong or absent. Go back to step 2. A list that cannot be cut is a list with nothing to measure against, and no amount of pushing at step 3 will fix a problem that lives at step 2.

---

## The courage part

Real focus means saying no to ideas you believe are excellent.

Saying no to bad ideas is not focus — that is just judgement, and it costs nothing. Focus is declining something you are genuinely excited about because it is not this mission, today. That is the part that feels bad, and the feeling is the signal that it is working.

**If nothing was painful to cut, the cut did not happen.** The user reshuffled. It is worth saying so plainly rather than accepting a comfortable list — a comfortable list is the failure mode, not the success state.

---

## Sizing the Signal list

Three to five, and the range carries information.

**Three** when the items are large or the day is fragmented by meetings. Three finished beats five attempted, every time.

**Five** when the items are small and independent.

**Never six.** Not because five is magic, but because the cap is what forces the ranking, and a cap that flexes under pressure stops functioning immediately. A cap that can be argued up to six can be argued up to nine.

**One** is legitimate and occasionally correct. A single item that consumes the whole horizon and genuinely moves the mission is a good day. Do not pad it to three to make the list look respectable.
