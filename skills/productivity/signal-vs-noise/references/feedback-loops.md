# The feedback loop

The feedback check. Raise it when work is shipping steadily but nothing is being learned — the specific feeling of working hard for months with nothing compounding.

---

## The loop

**Produce → publish → attend to the data → repeat.**

Most people run three of the four. They produce, they publish, they repeat — and skip the attending. That version feels identical from the inside and produces nothing, which is why it survives so long: effort is high, output is visible, and the absence of learning is invisible until someone asks what changed.

The third step is where knowledge is made. Without it, cycle ten is cycle one repeated ten times.

---

## Attending to the data

**Decide what you are watching before you publish.** Chosen afterwards, the metric that gets attention is the one that happened to look good — and that is a mechanism for feeling successful rather than becoming successful.

**Watch what people did, not what they said.** Compliments are unreliable and freely given; returning, paying, using, and recommending are not. The gap between "people said they loved it" and "nobody came back" is where most projects quietly die.

**One number per cycle.** More than that and everything can be explained as progress on something, which is indistinguishable from no signal.

**Look for the surprise.** Data confirming what you already believed changed nothing. Data that contradicts you is the only kind that pays, and the instinct to explain it away is worth resisting deliberately, because that instinct is strong and it feels like rigour.

---

## Cycle length

The loop must be short enough that the connection between action and result stays visible.

A quarterly loop teaches almost nothing: too much changed in between to attribute anything, and by the time results arrive the reasoning behind the decision has been forgotten. A daily loop on something that takes a month to show effect is noise mistaken for signal.

The rule: **the cycle should be as short as the shortest signal you can actually read.** If a change takes two weeks to show up, a two-week cycle is right and a two-day cycle is self-deception.

---

## Does this serve a stranger?

The hardest and most useful test for anything produced for an audience.

> Would this earn the attention of someone who has no reason to care about you?

Not your peers, who will be kind. Not your existing audience, who already decided. A stranger, with no obligation, competing offers everywhere, and no patience.

Work made for oneself is not wasted — it is just not the same activity, and the confusion between the two is what produces years of output with no audience. The tell is content whose value is legible only to someone already inside the context: the process post that assumes you follow the project, the feature that solves the author's workflow, the update written to feel productive.

Three sharpenings:

- **Name the specific person**, not "developers" or "founders". A real one, with a real problem, whose situation you can describe in a sentence. Value is concrete or it is not value.
- **What do they get out of the first ten seconds?** If the answer requires context they do not have, it is being written for someone else.
- **Would they pay — with money, attention, or a recommendation?** All three are real prices. Something nobody would pay any of them is not delivering value, whatever it feels like from the inside.

---

## Connecting back to the cut

The loop is what keeps Signal honest over time. Without it, "what moves the mission" stays a guess, and the same guess gets re-made every morning with increasing confidence and no new information.

With it, each cycle's review feeds the next cut: the thing that worked earns more Signal, the thing that did nothing gets deferred or deleted rather than repeated out of momentum.

This is why step 6 comes before the next step 1. **A cut made without reading the last cycle's results is a cut made on yesterday's beliefs** — and the whole point of the ritual is that the beliefs are supposed to be updating.
