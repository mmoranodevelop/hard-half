# The seven filters

Not phases. A candidate that fails one filter does not get rescued by the others. Filters 1, 2, and 5 are multiplicative with adoption: a zero on any of them makes net value zero.

Score each **pass / fail / unknown**. Unknown is allowed. Invented pass is not.

## 1 — Real problem (relevance)

*Does it exist for real people, and hurt enough to justify a change?*

Indicators of a real problem:

- people already spend time, money, or effort on a workaround
- it is frequent, intense, or both
- they recognize it when you name it — you do not have to explain that they have it
- it is *their* problem, not a problem you wish they had

Typical failure: a solution looking for a problem. Elegant technology, nobody in enough pain to change a habit.

Cause vs projection: "I have this problem" is a starting clue, not evidence. If nobody else recognizes it unprompted, you are projecting.

Jobs-to-be-done interviews (switch stories, hire/fire) are the method for this filter when the job is unclear. They are not a substitute for filters 2–7. If the user only wants the job story, stop and use `jobs-to-be-done`.

Checklist:

- [ ] Who, exactly? A nameable segment, not "everyone"
- [ ] How often, how much it hurts, what they do today to get around it
- [ ] Have you spoken to enough of them that something unexpected showed up? What was it?

## 2 — Efficacy

*Does the solution change the state of the problem, measurably?*

Define "resolved" and the measure **before** scoring the solution. Distinguish symptom from cause — both can have value, but only if declared.

Value is **differential**: how much better than the best alternative they already have, including doing nothing.

Typical failure: works in the demo, not in the person's actual week.

Checklist:

- [ ] What "resolved" means, and how it is measured
- [ ] Best current alternative, and how much better, in numbers or a named hole
- [ ] Cause or symptom — declared

## 3 — Validation

*Have 1 and 2 been checked with real people, or do you just believe them?*

A problem that has gone unsolved for a thousand years is **more** suspicious, not less. Either (a) people will not change for it, or (b) cost has always beaten benefit, or (c) prior attempts failed for reasons you do not yet know. Validation is how you find out which before you burn years.

Minimum cycle:

1. Write the hypothesis so it can die: "We believe [who] has [problem] and would use [solution] because [reason]; we will know if [measure] within [time]."
2. Build the smallest thing that can test it.
3. Put it in front of real people in the real segment.
4. Measure behavior, not opinions (what they do, not what they say).
5. Persevere, correct, or abandon.

Typical failure: team, friends, or investors treated as validation.

Checklist:

- [ ] Falsifiable hypothesis
- [ ] Smallest experiment that can kill it
- [ ] What was measured as real behavior

## 4 — Communication

*Do the right people know it exists, understand what it does for them, and trust it enough to try?*

Three levels, all required:

- **Awareness** — they know you exist
- **Understanding** — in ten seconds, which problem, for whom. If the promise needs a manual, the promise is wrong
- **Trust** — a reason to believe (proof, references, a low-risk try)

Honest communication multiplies realized value. Deceptive communication is a debt: churn, reputation, regulation.

Ask: is there something in the pitch that, if fully understood, would make the user walk away? If yes, you are inflating, not communicating.

Checklist:

- [ ] One sentence a person with the problem recognizes instantly
- [ ] What proof you offer
- [ ] The sentence that, if believed completely, would make them refuse

## 5 — Access and adoption

*Can the person who has the problem actually get and use the solution?*

- **Economic** — total cost under perceived value, inside what they can pay
- **Practical** — distribution, compatibility, language, required skill, time to learn
- **Behavioral** — how much must they change? Habit change is a huge cost and the one most often left off the page

Typical failure: perfect solution, switching cost (learn, migrate, convince others) larger than the benefit.

Checklist:

- [ ] Price plus total cost of use, under perceived value for *that* segment
- [ ] Habit-change cost, counted

## 6 — Sustainability

*Can it keep existing and working?*

- **Economic** — the way you capture value covers the cost of keeping it created. A free service that dies in a year destroyed the value of everyone who built on it
- **Operational** — maintenance, dependencies, key people. Who is alive in five years?
- **Scale** — at 100× users, does value degrade (congestion) or improve (network)?

Typical failure: subsidized growth the model cannot hold when the subsidy ends.

Checklist:

- [ ] How this pays for itself in three years
- [ ] What breaks at 100×

## 7 — Integrity

*Who pays the costs that are not on your books?*

This is the filter that turns "value for me" into "value in the world."

- Who is **not** at the table and still absorbs effects? (future users, third parties, environment, community, later generations)
- Does the business model earn when the user is better off, or when they are worse, hooked, or locked?
- If everyone used this, would the result be desirable? (universalisability)
- If the benefit vanished tomorrow, what damage would remain? (asymmetry)

Typical failure: optimizing a metric (engagement, revenue, speed) that has detached from the wellbeing it was supposed to stand for.

Checklist:

- [ ] Who is absent from the table, and what they absorb
- [ ] You earn when they are better, or when they are worse
- [ ] If everyone did this, would the world be better
- [ ] What remains if the benefit disappears
